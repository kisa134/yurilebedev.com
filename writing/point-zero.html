Point zero: what I carried with me

I have started over more times than I can count. This time I want to write down what I'm bringing into the new start.

CogitoOctober 20264 min read

I'm 29. I trade for a living, I build software, and I've just moved my life and work to Dubai. A new company, a new home, a new daily routine. I think of this moment as point zero. Not because everything before it was wasted, but because I'm deliberately choosing what to carry forward.

So before writing about markets, tools, or products, I want to write down what the past years actually taught me. It's less of a success story and more of an inventory.

Ideas were never the problem

I have kept a task manager since 2019. When I looked at it recently, I found thousands of completed tasks and more than forty archived projects. Some of those projects worked. Many didn't. A few came back again and again. This blog, for example, has been an idea every year or two, and every time it lost to something more urgent.

Looking at that archive was uncomfortable, but also clarifying. I never lacked ideas or energy. What I lacked was a structure that could hold them. Starting was easy for me. Finishing needed a system.

Motivation starts things. Structure finishes them.

Willpower is a bad foundation

For a long time I believed that if I just wanted something badly enough, I would do it. That works for a week. Then life gets loud, and whatever depended on willpower quietly stops.

What actually changed my days were boring things: a fixed time to work, a fixed time to rest, a short list of what matters today, and a weekly review of what happened. When a habit has a time and a place, I don't need to negotiate with myself every morning.

Speed is not the same as progress

I'm someone who rushes. For years I treated speed as a virtue: answer faster, decide faster, start the next thing faster. It felt like progress. Often it was just noise.

The hardest skill I'm still learning is slowing down on purpose. Not every opportunity is mine. Not every message needs an answer in five minutes. The thought "I could have..." is not a plan. It's usually just anxiety wearing the mask of ambition.

Thinking from first principles

At some point I started studying systems thinking seriously, and later the First Principles Framework. I don't use it as a philosophy to quote. I use it as a set of questions that I ask before an important decision.

  • What exactly am I trying to change, and how will I know it changed?
  • Who is doing what? Am I the right person for this part, or should someone else do it?
  • Is this a fact, or a guess I've started treating as a fact?
  • What would make me change my mind?

These questions sound simple. In practice they save me from a lot of confident mistakes. They also help me separate what only I can do from what I can delegate. That turned out to be one of the most useful distinctions of all.

Shocks are part of the system

This year, an exchange I used froze my account for more than a month. Nothing I did could speed it up. Plans I had made assumed it couldn't happen.

It taught me something I had only understood in theory: resilience is not a mood, it's a design decision. Don't keep everything in one place. Keep a reserve you don't touch. Build plans that survive a bad month, not just a good one. Now part of every week's result goes into a reserve outside the markets. It's not exciting. That's the point.

Work in public, think in writing

Most of my thinking used to live in private notes and chats. It helped me, but nobody else could see it, argue with it, or build on it. And honestly, without an audience, it was too easy to leave things half-finished.

Writing in public is my way of closing that loop. Here I'll share how I build my trading terminal, how I make decisions, what works and what doesn't. Not as advice, and not as a performance. Just a clear record of the work.

What I'm carrying into point zero

  • A daily structure that doesn't depend on mood.
  • A habit of asking first-principles questions before big decisions.
  • A reserve, and plans that assume things can break.
  • Fewer projects, finished properly.
  • Permission to slow down.
  • A place to write it all down. This one.

Everything before this moment was the training. Now I want to see what this system can build.
